Alternative ML: analog circuits

In a digital chip, most of the energy goes into moving numbers between memory and arithmetic units, one clock tick at a time. Analog machine learning keeps the numbers where they live and lets physics do the arithmetic, continuously and in parallel.

Multiply–add for free

Store each weight as a conductance \(G_{ij}\), for example a resistor or a memristor at a wire crossing. Put the inputs on the rows as voltages \(V_i\). Ohm's law multiplies (\(I = G\,V\)) and Kirchhoff's law adds the currents meeting on each column:

$$I_j \;=\; \sum_i G_{ij}\, V_i$$

That's a whole matrix–vector product in one step, right where the weights are stored. Drive the columns instead of the rows and the same array computes the transpose. That will matter in a moment.

Let the circuit settle into the answer

Physical circuits relax into states that balance their currents, which means they minimise an energy-like quantity. If you design a circuit whose energy is a learning model's energy, then simply waiting is the computation. That's exactly the shape of predictive coding, and of related ideas like equilibrium propagation.

A predictive coding neuron in three parts

Here is the simplest case. One hidden cause \(x\) explains one sensor reading \(y\) through one weight \(w\), with a prior that keeps \(x\) small:

$$E(x) \;=\; \tfrac12\,(\underbrace{y - w\,x}_{\varepsilon})^2 \;+\; \tfrac12\,x^2$$

Kirchhoff's current law at the capacitor gives the dynamics directly:

$$C\,\frac{dx}{dt} \;=\; \underbrace{w\,\varepsilon}_{\text{error current in}} \;-\; \underbrace{x/R}_{\text{leak}} \;=\; -\frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\qquad(R=1)$$

So the circuit performs gradient descent on the predictive-coding energy, with no clock and no processor. It comes to rest exactly where the currents balance:

$$x^\star \;=\; \frac{w\,y}{1 + w^2},$$

the best (maximum-a-posteriori) estimate of the cause.

Making the circuit learn

Settling finds the best belief for one reading. Learning means changing the weight itself, so that predictions improve across many readings. It's gradient descent on the same energy, this time with respect to the weight:

$$\tau_w\,\frac{dw}{dt} \;=\; -\frac{\partial E}{\partial w} \;=\; \underbrace{\varepsilon\,x}_{\text{Hebbian}} \;-\; \underbrace{\alpha\,w}_{\text{leak}}$$

The update needs only two signals, and both are already at the weight: the error on its row and the belief on its column. Their product nudges the weight so that the prediction moves towards the reading. A slow leak (a small prior \(\tfrac{\alpha}{2} w^2\) added to \(E\)) keeps it bounded. \(\tau_w\) is much slower than the capacitor, so the circuit infers on every reading and learns across many.

One sensor doesn't leave much to learn, so the circuit below gets a second one. A single cause now predicts two readings, \(\hat y = w\,x\) with \(w = (w_1, w_2)\). The two weights sit where the error rows cross the belief column, the crossbar of Fig. 1 in miniature, and each crossing adjusts its own conductance from the voltages on its row and column.

Where does it settle? The arrow ends up along the direction in which the readings vary most: the data's first principal component, the same answer PCA gives. The leak removes any direction whose variance is smaller than \(\alpha\), and only the main axis survives. The circuit finds it from local signals, with no clock and no processor. Real conductances can't go negative, so a signed weight is built from a pair of devices whose difference is used.

Scaling up

  • Replace the single ×w amplifiers with crossbars. One direction computes the predictions \(W x\). Driven the other way, the same conductances send the errors back up as \(W^{\top}\varepsilon\). Predictive coding's top-down and bottom-up pathways share one physical array.
  • Learning is the rule from Fig. 3 at every crossing at once: \(\Delta W \propto \varepsilon\, x^{\top}\), the outer product of the row and column signals. Overlapping voltage pulses on rows and columns apply it to the whole array in one step.
  • Analog hardware is noisy, with device variability, drift and limited precision. Energy-based models are forgiving here, and sampling-based predictive coding even needs noise to explore its beliefs.
The appeal is efficiency: the inference step costs the energy of letting charge settle, instead of shuttling numbers through a processor billions of times.
