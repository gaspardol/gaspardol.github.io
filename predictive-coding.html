Research interest 01

Predictive coding

Your brain isn't a camera; it's a prediction machine. Each level of the cortex guesses what the level below should be seeing, and only the surprise, the prediction error, needs to travel. Learning means getting surprised less often.

The energy machine

Picture every layer of a network as a bead on a rail. The layer above holds a guess of where that bead should be (the dashed circle). When guess and bead disagree, a spring between them stretches. Add up the tension in all the springs and you get the network's energy: a single number that says how surprised the network is.

What just happened, in four steps

  1. Predict. Each layer sends a guess downwards: the bead above, multiplied by a weight \(w\).
  2. Compare. Wherever a guess is off, an error appears, \(\varepsilon = x - w\,x_{\text{above}}\). That's a stretched spring.
  3. Relax (inference). The free beads move downhill on the energy. Each one only feels its own spring and the springs it pulls on just below. Nothing global is needed.
  4. Learn. Once things settle, each weight changes by error below × activity above. Next time, the same input leaves the springs looser. It's a local, Hebbian rule, and there's no separate backward pass.

The same thing in three lines

For a network whose layer \(\ell+1\) predicts layer \(\ell\) through weights \(W_\ell\) and a nonlinearity \(f\):

$$E \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{\ell} \big\lVert \underbrace{x_\ell - f(W_\ell\, x_{\ell+1})}_{\varepsilon_\ell} \big\rVert^2$$
$$\text{inference:}\quad \dot x_\ell \;=\; -\frac{\partial E}{\partial x_\ell} \;=\; -\,\varepsilon_\ell \;+\; W_{\ell-1}^{\top}\big(\varepsilon_{\ell-1}\odot f'(W_{\ell-1}x_\ell)\big)$$
$$\text{learning:}\quad \Delta W_\ell \;\propto\; -\frac{\partial E}{\partial W_\ell} \;=\; \big(\varepsilon_\ell \odot f'(W_\ell x_{\ell+1})\big)\, x_{\ell+1}^{\top}$$

Every quantity in these updates sits right next to the synapse that uses it. That's the whole appeal.

Why I find it exciting

  • It's local. Neurons and synapses only use signals that are physically next to them. That's plausible for brains and natural for parallel, neuromorphic or analog hardware.
  • It's a close cousin of backprop. Under some conditions, its weight updates approximate or even match backpropagation's gradients (Whittington & Bogacz, 2017).
  • It's more than a classifier. The same energy can generate data, fill in missing parts, and represent uncertainty. Adding a little noise to inference turns it into sampling, which is the idea behind Monte Carlo predictive coding.
